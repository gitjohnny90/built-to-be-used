The one-page AI policy: a template for small business teams

Here's what's true on most small teams right now: your people are already using AI. Some openly, some quietly, some pasting things into chatbots that should never leave the building. The fix isn't a 40-page document from a law firm — nobody on a five-person crew reads 40 pages. It's one page that answers five questions. If your policy answers these five, it works; if it doesn't, the length won't save you.

Why one page

A policy only protects you if people actually know what it says. Long policies get signed and forgotten; a one-pager gets taped next to the coffee maker. There's a second reason: writing it short forces you to make the real decisions instead of hiding behind language like "employees should exercise appropriate judgment." Appropriate judgment about what? The five questions below are where the actual judgment lives.

Question 1: What's always fine?

Start with permission, not prohibition. List the uses anyone can do without asking: drafting emails and estimates, summarizing meeting notes, brainstorming, rewriting something to be clearer, researching how-to questions. Be concrete — "drafting customer texts: yes" beats "communication assistance."

Why this comes first: if your policy opens with restrictions, people read it as "AI is dangerous here" and go underground. Shadow AI use — people using it but hiding it — is the worst outcome, because you get all the risk and none of the visibility. A generous, specific "always fine" list brings usage into the open where you can actually manage it.

Question 2: What never goes into an AI tool?

This is the hard line, and it should be short and absolute. Typical entries for a small business: customer names and contact details, anything covered by an NDA, employee personal or medical information, passwords and financial account details, and anything you'd be uncomfortable seeing on a competitor's screen.

Why it matters: most consumer AI tools may use what you type to improve their systems, and even when they don't, you've still sent confidential data to a third party. Your client list pasted into a chatbot is a data handoff you can't take back. People aren't careless because they're bad — they're careless because nobody ever told them where the line is. Draw it once, in writing.

Question 3: What needs a human check before it ships?

AI output is a draft, not a deliverable. Name the categories where a human must review before anything goes out the door: prices and quotes, anything with legal or safety implications, claims about what your product or service can do, and anything that goes to a customer under your company's name.

Why it matters: AI tools state wrong things with total confidence — wrong numbers, invented details, outdated rules. The failure mode isn't that AI writes badly; it's that it writes convincingly. A quote with a hallucinated price is a real liability with your signature on it. The rule of thumb worth writing down: the person who sends it owns it, no matter what drafted it.

Question 4: When do we disclose AI use?

Decide in advance, not after someone asks. A workable small-team default: internal use needs no disclosure; customer-facing work follows the "no-pretending" rule — never claim something was handmade when it wasn't, and answer honestly if asked. Some teams go further and disclose proactively on creative or advisory work.

Why it matters: getting caught hiding AI use costs more trust than the AI use itself ever would. Customers mostly don't mind that you used a tool; they mind being misled. Whatever line you choose, choose it together and write it down — a team where each person improvises their own ethics on this will eventually contradict each other in front of a customer.

Question 5: Who do we ask when we're not sure?

Name one person. Not a committee, not "leadership" — a name. Their job isn't to be the AI expert; it's to be the place uncertainty goes instead of into a guess.

Why it matters: every policy has gaps, and new tools and new situations will show up monthly. Without a named person, gaps get resolved silently by whoever hits them first, and you find out about the bad calls later. With one, gaps become questions, questions become precedents, and your one page quietly gets smarter. This is also how the policy stays alive instead of becoming the laminated thing nobody's read since onboarding.

Putting it on the page

The format that works: five headings matching the five questions, bullet points under each, plain language throughout, a date at the top, and a line at the bottom for each team member to sign. Then — and this is the part most teams skip — walk through it together in one 20-minute meeting where people can push back. A policy people helped edit is a policy people follow. Revisit it quarterly; it'll need it.

One more honesty note: don't write rules you won't enforce, and don't ban things you (the owner) do yourself. Your team learns the real policy by watching you.
