New homeowner? Here's what to do in your first year, month by month

Nobody hands you an owner's manual at closing. Yet almost every expensive repair I see as a handyman started as a cheap problem in year one that nobody was looking for. This is the first-year checklist I'd give a friend: what to do each month, why it matters, and what's safe to ignore.

Month 1 — Learn the house before you decorate it

  • Find your three shutoffs: water main, gas valve, electrical panel. Walk to each one, touch it, and tape a label on it. At 2 a.m. with water coming through a ceiling, you do not want to be learning the basement layout.
  • Change every lock and garage code. You have no idea how many copies of the old keys exist.
  • Test smoke and CO detectors, and note their dates. Detectors over 10 years old (7 for many CO units) should be replaced, not just re-batteried.
  • Change the HVAC filter and buy a year's supply. It's the cheapest thing you can do for the most expensive system you own.
  • Label your electrical panel if the previous owner didn't. One evening with a helper and a lamp.

Month 2 — Paperwork and baselines

  • Start a house binder or folder: inspection report, appliance manuals, paint colors, filter sizes, and a page for every service visit. Future-you (and the next buyer) will thank you.
  • Read your inspection report again. Now that you live there, the flagged items will make sense. Sort them into "fix this year," "watch," and "cosmetic."
  • Take photos of every room and the mechanicals. It's your insurance baseline and your "what did that corner look like before the stain?" reference.

Month 3 — The water tour

  • Look under every sink with a flashlight. You're hunting for slow drips, corrosion on the supply lines, and soft spots in the cabinet floor. Five minutes per sink.
  • Check the water heater: note its age from the serial number, look for rust at the base, and confirm nothing is stored against it. Tanks typically last 8–12 years; knowing yours is 11 changes your planning.
  • Run every faucet and flush every toilet you don't normally use. Traps dry out and seals fail quietly in unused bathrooms.

Month 4 — Exterior walkaround

  • Walk the entire outside slowly and look at where the ground meets the house. Soil should slope away from the foundation; downspouts should discharge several feet from the wall. Bad drainage is how basements get expensive.
  • Scan the roofline from the ground with binoculars: lifted shingles, sagging gutters, gaps in flashing around chimneys and vents. You're not fixing anything — you're catching it small.
  • Trim anything touching the house. Branches abrade shingles and shrubs against siding trap moisture.

Month 5 — Seals and air

  • Inspect caulk around tubs, showers, and sinks. Cracked caulk lets water into walls and subfloors, and recaulking is one of the highest-payoff DIY jobs there is.
  • Check weatherstripping on exterior doors. If you can see daylight, you're paying to heat or cool the neighborhood.
  • Clean the dryer vent — the duct, not just the lint screen. Lint-clogged ducts are a real fire risk and make every load take longer.

Month 6 — Mid-year mechanical check

  • Test the sump pump if you have one: pour in a bucket of water and make sure it kicks on. Before the wet season, not during.
  • Vacuum refrigerator coils and check the drain pan. Ten minutes, longer appliance life.
  • Test GFCI outlets (the ones with the little buttons, in kitchens, baths, garage) using the test/reset buttons.

Months 7–9 — Seasonal turn

  • Clean the gutters once the leaves are down (or before the heavy rain season in your area). A clogged gutter overflows against the fascia and foundation — this is the classic cheap-problem-becomes-foundation-issue story.
  • Service the heating system before the first cold snap — or at minimum run it for an hour on a mild day so a failure shows up when it's 60°F out, not 10°F.
  • Drain and disconnect garden hoses before first freeze and shut off exterior spigots if your house has interior valves for them. Burst hose bibs flood wall cavities.
  • Flush the water heater once this year. Sediment buildup is the main reason tanks die early, and the flush takes about 30 minutes.

Months 10–12 — Close the loop

  • Re-walk the inspection report one last time. Anything in the "fix this year" pile still open? Schedule it before it ages into the "wish I had" pile.
  • Review your maintenance log and set the recurring calendar reminders for year two: filters monthly-ish, gutters and roofline twice a year, water heater flush annually.
  • Build the small emergency kit: headlamp, water shutoff tool if your main needs one, a few towels in a known spot, and the plumber's and electrician's numbers saved in your phone before you need them at emergency rates.

When to call a pro instead

Everything above is homeowner-grade. The honest line: anything inside the electrical panel, anything involving the gas line, structural changes, and roof work beyond looking at it from the ground are pro calls. A good rule for year one — if a mistake could flood, burn, or collapse something, you're buying judgment, not just labor.
