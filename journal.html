Building in public: the Agent Income Test

I'm John — a working handyman in Colorado. This whole store is an experiment I'm running in the open: can a regular guy with a trade, a full-time schedule, and a set of AI systems build something that earns real money? This page is the honest journal of how it's going. Including the part where, so far, it's earning nothing.

The setup: June 10, 2026, evening

The experiment started with a question and some research. Everywhere you look, someone's claiming AI agents printed them money overnight. When we actually dug into those stories, the pattern was less magical: most of the viral wins were hype monetized through an existing audience — the money came from being famous for making money. The repeatable, unglamorous wins came from a different place: marketplaces where buyers already are.

So we locked a plan that fit the boring version of the truth: digital products on Gumroad, plus free landing pages to catch search traffic. The overnight goal: six or more genuinely useful products — created, published, and findable by morning. I did the one thing only a human could do (created the Gumroad account and connected payouts), set the direction and the standards, and let my AI systems build through the night while I slept. That division of labor isn't a gimmick; it's the actual point of the test.

The overnight: June 10–11

By late evening on the 10th, the first product was done — a backyard chicken record binder, of all things, 19 pages, checked page by page. By the deep of the night, five of six were finished: the homeowner maintenance system, an AI prompt playbook for tradesmen, a Christian game night pack, a pet sitter binder, and a long-distance date night pack.

One thing I care about got special treatment that night: an integrity sweep. Early drafts had lines like "written by a handyman" and "I held the pen on every word." That's not true — I didn't hold the pen, I held the standards — so every overclaim got rewritten to the honest framing you'll find on the last page of every PDF: made by John and his AI systems, openly. If this experiment works, I want it to work without lying about how.

Around midnight, the store went live: six products, publicly buyable. Around 12:30 a.m., this website went live too (on GitHub Pages, because the first hosting choice fell through — there's a decision log for all of this).

The 1 a.m. pivot

At about 1 a.m. I redirected the plan: alongside the printables, we'd build take-home mini-courses — each one a single interactive HTML file that works offline, needs no login, saves your progress inside the file itself, and is yours forever. "Productivity in the Age of AI" went live first, then "Leadership & Character in the AI Wild West" around 1:30 a.m. The printables stayed up as the control group. Yes, I was making product strategy decisions at 1 a.m. No, I don't fully recommend it. It was fun.

The morning scoreboard: June 11, 5:45 a.m.

Eight products live. A working store with payouts connected. A landing site on the public internet. Roughly 25 git commits and a decision log. Sales, checked hourly through the night: zero.

And honestly? Zero was the expected number. The research said the median first dollar for a cold-start store takes weeks to months. Our two real traffic channels hadn't even started: Google takes days to index a new site, and Gumroad's Discover marketplace only includes you after your first sale and a risk review — a catch-22 that every new seller faces. The machine got built overnight. Eyeballs were never going to be overnight. The morning conclusion, verbatim from the log: "traffic needs a human." Reddit posts, Pinterest, telling friends — the AI can draft those, but it rightly isn't allowed to pretend to be me, so that part is on me.

Later on June 11: ten products and one mystery wall

By that afternoon, two more courses were live — The Crew Code (team building in the age of AI) and The Modern Tradesman (running a handyman business) — and I repriced all the courses from $35 down to $19. Ten products live, $108 of total list price.

Then came my favorite plot twist so far: an eleventh product, fully built and verified, that Gumroad silently refuses to publish. The form just bounces — no error, no explanation. Best guess: a soft cap on new accounts until the first sale or a risk review clears. We deliberately stopped retrying, because hammering a marketplace limit is exactly how new accounts get flagged. So there's a finished course sitting on the bench, waiting for the store's first dollar to unlock the door. The irony is not lost on me.

What this experiment is actually testing

Not "can AI make money while you sleep" — the internet has enough of those claims. The real questions: Can AI systems plus one human's judgment produce work that's genuinely worth paying for? Can it be done with total honesty about who made what? And can it find buyers without an existing audience, faked reviews, or hype? Two of those three look promising. The third is the whole game, and the scoreboard currently reads zero.

I'll keep updating this page as the experiment continues — first sale, first failure, first lesson worth writing down. If the whole thing flops, that gets written here too. That's the deal with building in public: the public part doesn't pause when the news is bad.
