How to price handyman jobs: the hourly rate calculator you can run on paper

Most new handymen price the same way: check what the guy down the road charges, knock a little off to win work, and call it a rate. That's not pricing — that's copying someone else's mistakes at a discount. Here's the honest way to find your number: work out your hourly floor from your own costs, then never quote below it.

Why underpricing kills handyman businesses

Underpricing doesn't feel like dying. It feels like winning — your phone rings, your calendar fills, you're "busy." But busy at a losing rate means every job quietly costs you money once the truck, the insurance, the unpaid quote visits, and the tax bill are counted. You can't make it up on volume; volume just makes you lose money faster, with more wear on your body and your truck. The cheap guys you're tempted to match are mostly running the same math-free spiral — and most of them won't be in business in three years to compete with you anyway.

There's a second cost: cheap prices attract the customers who fight every invoice, and they repel the ones who happily pay for reliability. Your price is a signal, and "suspiciously cheap" is a signal too.

The hourly floor: the one number to know cold

Your hourly floor is the rate at which a billed hour actually pays you what you need — after overhead and taxes, across only the hours you can really bill. The formula fits on an index card:

Hourly floor = (what you need to earn + taxes + overhead) ÷ true billable hours per year

Two parts of that trip everyone up.

True billable hours are fewer than you think

You don't bill 40 hours a week. Quotes, driving, supply runs, invoicing, callbacks, and slow weeks eat roughly a third to half of your time, and none of it is billable. A solo operator working full weeks can realistically bill somewhere around 20–25 hours a week — plan with that, not with fantasy.

The truck-day cost: what it costs just to show up

Before you turn a single screw, the day already cost you money: insurance, fuel, vehicle wear, tool replacement, phone, software, marketing, licenses. Divide your annual overhead by your working days and you get your truck-day cost — the amount every working day must recover before you've earned a cent. Knowing this number is what makes "it's just a quick $40 job across town" obviously unprofitable.

From the floor to actual prices

  • Set a minimum job charge. Small jobs carry full drive time and truck-day cost, so most pros charge a first-hour or show-up minimum well above the plain hourly rate. It filters out the jobs that lose money and nobody serious blinks at it.
  • Quote flat-rate where you know the work cold. Once you've hung twenty ceiling fans, price the job, not the hours: estimate honestly, multiply by your floor, add margin for the surprises that always come. Customers prefer a firm number, and your speed becomes profit instead of a pay cut.
  • Half-day and full-day rates beat hour-counting for punch lists. A day of your work has a value; selling it in one block kills the meter-watching dynamic.
  • Mark up materials. Sourcing, hauling, and warrantying materials is work. A modest standard markup isn't gouging — it's billing for the part of the job that happens at the supply house.
  • Raise prices with your calendar. Booked solid two-plus weeks out is your market telling you you're underpriced. Raise rates on new quotes and let the bottom slice of the work go to the cheap guys.

Saying the number out loud

The math is the easy half. The hard half is quoting your floor to a real person without flinching — and that's a confidence skill you build, not a personality trait you're born missing. The script that helps: you're not charging for the hour, you're charging for the ten years that make the hour look easy, plus the insurance, the warranty, and showing up when you said you would. The customer who only wants the cheapest guy was never your customer.
